You are not a goldfish

Short courses are sold on the idea that our attention span has shrunk below a goldfish’s. Nobody ever measured that. The real case for short sessions is stranger: they work because they make learning feel worse.

You have heard it. The average human attention span used to be twelve seconds; now it is eight, one second less than a goldfish. It turns up in keynotes, in marketing decks and in nearly every pitch for “bite-sized” learning.

It was never measured. The numbers appeared in a 2015 Microsoft Canada report for advertisers, credited to a statistics website. When the BBC went looking for the research behind them, it found none, and the website did not answer.1 A researcher the BBC asked put it simply: attention depends on the task. The person who cannot get through a long email will read a novel to the end on holiday.

So if short courses only made sense because we had turned into goldfish, they would make no sense at all. They do make sense, for a reason that sounds backwards. Short sessions spread over days work because they make learning harder, and the four findings behind that are among the most surprising in the psychology of memory.

Surprise one: forgetting helps you learn

The psychologists Elizabeth and Robert Bjork put it bluntly: when something is fully accessible in memory, “little or no learning results from additional instruction or practice.” Forgetting is what “creates the opportunity” to learn it more deeply.2 Practice is worth most just when it has become a little hard.

The numbers are large. In a study of more than 1,300 people tested a year later, the best gap between two study sessions was about four weeks, long enough to start forgetting. For the same total study time, it gave 77% better recall than studying twice in a row.3 A review of 839 comparisons from 317 experiments found the spacing effect again and again.4 A course of short sessions on separate days builds the gap in. You cannot cram it, because tomorrow’s session is not there yet.

Surprise two: mixing things up works better

Students practised formulas for the volumes of different solids, either one kind at a time or shuffled together. A week later, the shuffled group solved 63% of new problems; the tidy group, 20%. In another study, people learned to recognise the styles of twelve painters. Seeing the painters mixed beat seeing each painter’s works in a row, yet most participants were sure the opposite was true.2 A short session that brings yesterday’s material back among today’s is mixing by design.

Surprise three: getting it wrong first helps

People asked questions about a text before reading it learned more than people given extra time to read it, and the gain showed on exactly the questions they had answered wrong.5 A wrong guess is not wasted. It prepares the ground for the answer. That is why a good session starts with a question, not a lecture.

Surprise four: feeling you’ve learned it is a bad sign

In a classic study, students read a short text. Some reread it three more times, others tried three times to write down what they remembered. Five minutes later the rereaders were ahead, 83% to 71%. A week later the order had flipped: 61% for those who had recalled, 40% for those who had reread. The rereaders had felt more confident.6 In a flashcard study, spacing beat cramming for 90% of the participants, and after the first session 72% of them believed cramming had worked better.7

The Bjorks’ summary: conditions “that make performance improve rapidly often fail to support long-term retention.”2 No wonder a large review rated spaced practice and self-testing the most useful study techniques, and rereading and highlighting, the ones so many of us rely on, among the least.8 Learning that feels smooth often isn’t. Willpower is a poor tool against a feeling that strong; a format is a better one, because it decides once.

So is short the point? No.

Short is only the delivery, and the same research sets its limits. The Bjorks stress the word desirable: a difficulty helps only if you can meet it, otherwise it is just hard.2 Most of the evidence comes from things you can remember; an older review summarised in the 2006 one found a spacing benefit in only a third of the studies on intellectual skills such as arithmetic.4 Skills grow through practice with feedback, and feedback can backfire: in a review of 607 effects, more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse, most often when the feedback turned attention from the task to the person.9 And short formats have a failure all of their own: getting very good at bringing you back every day without making you any better.

What we think a short course owes you

  • It comes back after you have started to forget, not while it is still fresh.
  • It mixes yesterday’s material in with today’s.
  • It asks before it tells, and lets you be wrong.
  • It ends on something you made, with good examples to check it against, so feedback is about the work, not about you.
  • It measures the work, not the visits. Did the last piece, made cold, beat the first?
