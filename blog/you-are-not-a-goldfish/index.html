You are not a goldfish

Short courses are sold on the claim that our attention has shrunk below a goldfish’s. Nobody can find where that came from. The better case is stranger: practice spread over days works because it feels harder, and that feeling is exactly what fools us.

●  TL;DR

  • The myth: nobody has found a measurement behind the “eight-second attention span”. It is no reason for short courses.
  • The real reason: short sessions make it easy to spread practice over days, mix things up and ask before telling. That feels harder, and it often works better.
  • The catch: because it feels harder, we take it for learning less. A good course makes those choices for you, and measures the work, not the visits.

You have heard it. The average human attention span used to be twelve seconds; now it is eight, one second less than a goldfish. It turns up in keynotes, in marketing decks and in nearly every pitch for “bite-sized” learning.

Nobody has found a measurement behind it. The numbers appeared in a 2015 report by Microsoft Canada’s consumer insights team, credited to a statistics website, and when the BBC looked for the research behind them, it found none. One psychologist told the BBC that attention depends on the task.1 Think of the person who cannot finish a long email but reads a novel to the end on holiday.

So if short courses made sense only because we had turned into goldfish, they would make none. They make sense for another reason, and it sounds backwards. Short sessions make it easy to spread practice over days, and practice that is spread out, mixed up and tested feels harder, yet often works better.

Forgetting helps you learn

When something is fully accessible in memory, write the psychologists Elizabeth and Robert Bjork, “little or no learning results from additional instruction or practice.” Forgetting is what “creates the opportunity”.2 In a study of more than 1,300 people, those tested a year later remembered most when their two study sessions had been three to four weeks apart: 77% more than people who spent the same time studying twice in a row.3

The right gap depends on how long you want to remember: a day for next week, weeks for next year.3 A course that brings material back later and later builds that in. And you cannot cram it, because tomorrow’s session is not there yet.

Mixing things up works better

Eighteen students practised formulas for the volumes of four solids, either one kind at a time or shuffled together. During practice, the one-at-a-time group did far better, 89% to 60%. A week later, the shuffled group solved 63% of new problems, the others 20%.4 Mixing helps most when things are easy to confuse, so that you learn to tell them apart, though the evidence is thinner than for spacing.5

Getting it wrong first helps

People asked questions about a text before reading it learned more than people given extra time to read, even counting only the questions they had got wrong.6 A wrong guess prepares the ground for the answer. The gain stayed with what was asked, though, so a good lesson opens with a question about what matters.

Why all of this feels wrong

Each of these makes practice feel worse while it is happening, and we judge practice by how it feels. In a classic study, some students reread a short text about fourteen times; others read it a few times and then tried, three times over, to write down what they remembered. Five minutes later, the rereaders were ahead, 83% to 71%. A week later it was 61% to 40% the other way, and the rereaders had been the more confident.7 In a flashcard study, nine in ten people did better with their cards spaced out, and after the first session most of them believed the opposite.8

Conditions “that make performance improve rapidly often fail to support long-term retention and transfer,” the Bjorks write.2 Learning that feels smooth often isn’t. Willpower loses to a feeling that strong. A format does not have to argue with it: the decision is made once, in advance.

So is short the point? No.

Short is only the delivery, and the research sets its limits. A difficulty helps only if you can meet it; otherwise it is just hard.2 Most of the evidence comes from things you can remember: an older review of 120 articles found a spacing benefit in only a third of the studies on skills such as arithmetic.9 Skills need practice with feedback, and feedback helps on average, but in a review of 607 effects more than a third of the interventions made performance worse, most often when they turned attention from the task to the person.10

And short formats have a failure all of their own: getting very good at bringing you back every day without making you any better.

What we think a short course owes you

  • It comes back after you have started to forget, and later each time.
  • It mixes things that are easy to confuse, so you learn to tell them apart.
  • It asks before it tells, and lets you be wrong.
  • It ends on something you made, with good examples to check it against.
  • Its feedback is about the work, not about you.
  • It measures the work, not the visits: does the latest thing you made, cold, beat the first?
