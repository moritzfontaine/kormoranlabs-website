You are not a goldfish

Short courses are sold on the claim that our attention has shrunk below a goldfish’s. Nobody can find where that came from. The better case is stranger: practice spread over days works because it feels harder, and that feeling is exactly what fools us.

You have heard it. The average human attention span used to be twelve seconds; now it is eight, one second less than a goldfish. It turns up in keynotes, in marketing decks and in nearly every pitch for “bite-sized” learning.

Nobody has found a measurement behind it. The numbers appeared in a 2015 report by Microsoft Canada’s consumer insights team, credited to a statistics website. When the BBC went looking for the research behind them, it found none, and the website did not answer. One psychologist told the BBC that attention depends on the task.1 Think of the person who cannot finish a long email but reads a novel to the end on holiday.

So if short courses made sense only because we had turned into goldfish, they would make none. They make sense for another reason, and it sounds backwards. Short sessions make it easy to spread practice over days, and practice that is spread out, mixed up and tested feels harder than the alternative, yet often works better. Three findings show how, and a fourth explains why the feeling fools us.

Forgetting helps you learn

The psychologists Elizabeth and Robert Bjork put it bluntly: when something is fully accessible in memory, “little or no learning results from additional instruction or practice.” Forgetting is what “creates the opportunity” to learn it more deeply.2 Practice is worth most once it has become a little hard.

The effect is large. In a study of more than 1,300 people, those tested a year later remembered most when their two study sessions had been three to four weeks apart: 77% more than people who spent the same time studying twice in a row.3 A review of 839 comparisons from 317 experiments found the spacing effect again and again.4

There is no one right gap, though. It depends on how long you want to remember: for a test a week away, a day apart did best; for a test a year away, weeks. The researchers warn that squeezing learning into too short a period produces “misleadingly high levels of immediate mastery.”3 Sessions on separate days build in a gap; a course that brings material back later and later builds in the right one. And you cannot cram it, because tomorrow’s session is not there yet.

Mixing things up works better

Eighteen students practised formulas for the volumes of four different solids, either one kind at a time or shuffled together. During practice, the one-at-a-time group did far better, 89% to 60%. A week later the order had reversed: the shuffled group solved 63% of new problems, the others 20%.5 In another study, people learned to recognise the styles of twelve painters. Seeing the painters mixed beat seeing each painter’s works in a row, yet most participants thought the row had worked as well or better.6

Mixing helps most when the things mixed are easy to confuse, so that you have to learn to tell them apart, and the evidence for it is thinner than for spacing: a large 2013 review rated it only moderately useful so far.7 A lesson that sets yesterday’s kind of problem beside today’s builds it in.

Getting it wrong first helps

People asked questions about a text before reading it learned more than people given extra time to read it. The advantage held even when the researchers counted only the questions people had got wrong, so it was not down to lucky guesses.8 A wrong guess is not wasted. It prepares the ground for the answer.

The benefit stayed with what was asked, though, and did not spread to the rest of the text. So the opening question has to be about what matters. That is why a good lesson starts with one, not with a lecture.

Why all of this feels wrong

Here is the catch that ties the three together. Each makes practice feel worse while it is happening, and we judge practice by how it feels.

In a classic study, students read a short text. Some spent three more five-minute periods rereading it, about fourteen readings in all. Others read it a few times and then spent those three periods writing down what they remembered, with no feedback. Five minutes later the rereaders were ahead, 83% to 71%. A week later the order had flipped: 61% for those who had recalled, 40% for those who had reread. The rereaders had been more confident.9 In a flashcard study, nine in ten people did better with their cards spaced out than studied in small batches, and after the first session most of them believed the batches had worked better.10

The Bjorks’ summary: conditions “that make performance improve rapidly often fail to support long-term retention and transfer.”2 No wonder the 2013 review rated spaced practice and self-testing the most useful study techniques, and rereading and highlighting, the ones so many of us rely on, among the least.7 Learning that feels smooth often isn’t.

Willpower loses to a feeling that strong. A format does not have to argue with it: the decision is made once, in advance, and not every evening.

So is short the point? No.

Short is only the delivery, and the research sets its limits. The Bjorks stress the word desirable: a difficulty helps only if you can meet it; otherwise it is just hard.2 And most of the evidence comes from things you can remember. An older review of 120 articles found a spacing benefit in only a third of the studies on skills such as arithmetic.4

Skills grow through practice with feedback, and feedback is not automatically good. Across 607 effects it helped on average, but more than a third of the feedback interventions made performance worse, most often when they turned attention from the task to the person.11

And short formats have a failure all of their own: getting very good at bringing you back every day without making you any better.

What we think a short course owes you

  • It comes back after you have started to forget, and later each time.
  • It mixes things that are easy to confuse, so you learn to tell them apart.
  • It asks before it tells, and lets you be wrong.
  • It ends on something you made, with good examples to check it against.
  • Its feedback is about the work, not about you.
  • It measures the work, not the visits: does the latest thing you made, cold, beat the first?
