Your AI wrote you a course. Then it got stuck in the chat.

Asking a model for a course takes a minute, and the result is often good. Then it stays where it was born: in a conversation, or on a page that never asks you anything again. Here is what that costs, and how Minute Studio takes the course out of the chat and turns it into practice, for you, for a few friends, or for everybody.

A course in a minute

Ask any assistant for “two weeks of sight-reading” or “how to ask for a raise” and you get a respectable course in seconds: units, explanations, a few questions, sometimes a quiz built as an interactive page beside the chat. For many subjects it is a better start than most of what a search turns up, and it is cut to you: your level, your goal, your fifteen minutes a day.

That is one of the genuinely useful things language models do. The trouble starts on day two.

Where the chat falls short

A chat is a fine place to write a course and a poor place to keep one. An artifact, the page a model builds next to the conversation, looks better but shares most of the same limits.

It never comes back

Most of what is known about learning that lasts comes down to three things: spacing, meeting the same material again across days; retrieval, producing an answer rather than reading one; and practice with feedback. A chat does the first only if you remember to come back, scroll up, find where you were and hope the thread still holds the context. Nothing asks you yesterday’s question tomorrow.

The answer arrives before the question

A model is built to be helpful, so it explains first and asks after, or answers its own question in the next paragraph. Ask it to quiz you and it marks you kindly: it is the same voice that wrote the lesson, and it wants the conversation to go well. In an artifact’s quiz, the answers sit in the page itself.

It changes every time you look

Regenerate and you get a different course. Come back next week and the model improvises from wherever the thread drifted. There is no fixed edition: nothing to correct once, translate, or hand to somebody else knowing they get what you got.

Your work disappears into the scroll

Many courses are about something you make: a text, a drawing, a budget, a plan. In a chat your attempts are messages among messages. The first version and the fifth never stand side by side, so you cannot see whether you got better.

Sharing means sending a transcript

A chat link shares a conversation, not a course. Whoever opens it finds your answers already given, no progress of their own and nothing that comes back. An artifact shares a page, and nobody has checked whether what it says is right. The best course somebody ever had a model write ends up in a thread nobody else reads.

Keep the model, move the course

Minute Studio is built on the idea that a course is something you practise, not something you scroll: a series of short sessions, a few minutes each, where each session asks before it explains and makes or improves a piece of your own work. Models turn out to be good at first drafts in exactly that shape. So we left the model where it is strong, drafting, and gave the course somewhere to live.

  1. Copy the brief. In the studio’s Source tab, Copy AI brief copies a description of the course language: how a unit is built, which kinds of question exist, what comes back. Paste it into any AI chat, describe the course you want underneath, and send.
  2. Paste the draft back. What comes back is plain text in that language. Paste it into the Source tab. If you have set up your own model and API key in your account, Draft with a model does both steps at once, from a topic or from an article you wrote.
  3. Cut it down on the board. The draft opens as cards you edit in place. The studio checks the course on every save and puts what needs fixing on the card where it is: a quiz with no answer marked right, a course in which nothing ever comes back, a question that returns weeks later but still says “above”.
  4. Try it, then publish it for yourself. Try this unit plays the session in a phone-sized frame. Publish for myself turns the draft into a course you take like any other.

One rule holds throughout: a model writes only what a person approves. Nothing a model drafts reaches a learner until somebody has read it and saved it, and there is no chatbot inside a session improvising new material while you learn.

New ways to learn what a model wrote

Once the draft is a course, the things a chat cannot do come with it.

  • Guess first, judged fairly. Questions are judged on the server. The answer is cut from the copy your browser receives and returns with the verdict, then the explanation of why. Neither the page nor a friendly model can mark you right.
  • Questions that come back. A question marked as a learning card returns on later days, its gap fitted to how you answered it, mixed in with the cards from your other courses. A wrong answer comes back first thing the next day until it is right.
  • Your work, in versions. A course can file a photo or a text into your folder each session. The folder keeps every version, the first beside the newest, with the changes in a text marked. You see whether you got better.
  • A rhythm that waits for you. A reminder at the time you choose. Missing a day loses nothing: what is due waits.
  • One sentence of feedback, if you want it. Where a course allows it, your own model gives one sentence on what you wrote, against the course’s own rubric, and it is stored nowhere. No model grades you, and none judges your work.

From one learner to a community

A course that works for you may work for others. Minute Studio has three circles for it, each a step further out.

  • Just you

    Publish for myself puts the course in your own studio, with progress, returning questions and your folder. Every plan includes courses of your own.

  • A few people you choose

    On the Studio plan, invite up to three people to each of your courses, by address or by link. Each walks every unit with their own progress, cards and folder, outside the catalogue.

  • Everybody

    Submit for review sends the course to a person who reads it session by session and leaves notes where they apply. Once approved it is in the catalogue, where anyone can try the first unit without an account.

Publishing to everybody costs nothing: you pay to keep a course to yourself, not to share it, and while a public course of yours is live you hold the Studio plan for free. Authors of catalogue courses share in what learners pay, split by the time learners spend in their courses, under a formula published before anyone is paid by it.

Review is the step a chat skips. A model’s confident mistake is harmless in one conversation and costly in a thousand, so before a course reaches strangers a person reads it as a learner would. While Minute Studio is in testing, catalogue submissions are opened account by account.

“Community” here does not mean a feed. What is shared is the course itself, not people: no followers, no leaderboards, no comments under a course. What learners see of each other is one figure, whether they would recommend a course, yes or no, shown as a share once ten have answered. The best course a model ever drafted becomes simply one of the best courses: cut down by a person, checked by another, with a promise you can check before you start.
