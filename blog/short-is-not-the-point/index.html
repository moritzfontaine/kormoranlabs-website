Short is not the point

Short courses are often sold on shrinking attention spans. That is the wrong reason. The right ones are older, better tested and more demanding.

Every few months someone repeats that our attention span has fallen below a goldfish's: eight seconds for us, nine for the fish. The figure appeared in a 2015 Microsoft Canada report for advertisers, credited to a statistics website. When the BBC went looking for the research behind it, there wasn't any.1 People who binge a whole series in a weekend do not have an eight-second attention span.

If short courses rested on that claim, they would rest on nothing. We think they rest on something much better, and that the better reason also says what a short course has to do to be worth anything.

Spacing: the same thing, met again later

The best-replicated finding in the psychology of memory is that study spread over time beats the same amount of study in one sitting. A 2006 review gathered 839 comparisons from 317 experiments and found the effect over and over.2 Two years later, the same group tested more than 1,300 people with gaps of up to three and a half months and final tests up to a year later. For a test a year out, the best gap between two sessions was about four weeks, and for the same total study time it gave 77% better recall than studying twice in a row.3

A course of short sessions on separate days is spaced by its shape. You cannot cram it, because tomorrow's session is not there yet.

Retrieval: answering instead of rereading

The second finding is that pulling something out of memory changes it more than putting it in again. In a classic study, students read a short text. Some reread it three more times, others tried three times to write down what they remembered. Five minutes later the rereaders were ahead, 83% to 71%. A week later the order had flipped: 61% for those who had recalled, 40% for those who had reread. The rereaders had also felt more confident.4

It even works before you know the answer. People asked questions about a text before reading it learned more than people given extra time to read, on exactly the questions they had got wrong.5 A guess primes you for the explanation. That is why a short session should start with a question, not with a lecture.

Why a format, and not just good advice

None of this is secret. A large 2013 review rated spaced practice and practice testing the two most useful study techniques it examined, and rereading and highlighting among the least.6 Yet people keep rereading, because it feels better. In one flashcard study, spacing beat cramming for 90% of the participants, and after the first session 72% of them believed cramming had worked better.7

Learning that feels smooth often isn't, and learning that works often feels like struggle. Willpower is a poor tool against a feeling that strong. A format is a better one: it decides once, so you don't have to decide every day.

Where short falls short

Here is the part the brochures leave out. Most of the evidence for spacing and retrieval comes from things you can remember: words, facts, rules, names. An earlier review of 120 articles, summarised in the 2006 one, found a spacing benefit in more than 80% of the studies on verbal material and on motor skills, but in only a third of those on intellectual skills such as arithmetic; half of those showed no effect at all.2 A skill is not a list of facts. It grows through practice with feedback, and feedback is no magic either: in a review of 607 effects, more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse, most often when the feedback turned attention away from the task and towards the person.8

And there is a failure that belongs to short formats alone: being excellent at bringing you back every day without making you better. A streak is easy to count and learning is not, so products drift towards the streak.

What we think a short course owes you

So short is not the point. It is the delivery, and it is only as good as what it delivers. Our view of a short course worth your minutes:

  • It comes back. What you learned returns on later days, at growing gaps, instead of being shown once.
  • It asks before it tells. A question first, then the why.
  • It ends on something you did. A drawing, a paragraph, an answer: your own work, not a page you scrolled past.
  • It gives you a standard to check against. Good examples and typical flaws, so feedback is about the work, not about you.
  • It measures the work, not the visits. Did the last piece, made cold, beat the first?
